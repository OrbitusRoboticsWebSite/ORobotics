## 1. The origin — cardboard before metal

**Suggested date:** September 8

**Suggested media:** a verified early-prototype photo if available; otherwise use
a later portrait and begin the caption with "Looking back..."

Looking back at where ROB began 🤖

The new treaded prototype dates to around 2016 in College Station, Texas. One of
the best early tools was not a mill or a welder—it was cardboard. The LT2 tread
system was the fixed starting point, so cheap full-size mockups helped answer the
important questions first: Does it fit? Can the battery come out? Will a moving
part clear the body?

ROB is not an acronym. The name simply grew out of the word *robot*. The machine
grew the same way: one question, one prototype, one lesson at a time.

## 2. 2019 — the whole idea moves

**Suggested date:** September 9

**Suggested media:** `social/reels/2019-rob-learned-to-move/rob-2019-recap.mp4`

2019: the whole idea was already moving.

Before the newer cameras, control software, and rebuilt mechanisms, ROB was
already tackling the coordination problem: a tracked base, a turning camera
head, and two articulated arms sharing one body. These workshop clips end with a
very maker-style experiment—supporting a planter across the arms.

It was not a polished product demo. It was a test. Every movement exposed a new
question about balance, load paths, wiring, timing, and control.

Seven years later, those lessons are part of our push toward Maker Faire Bay
Area at Mare Island, September 25–27.

## 3. 2020 — one robot, five cooperating systems

**Suggested date:** September 10

**Suggested media:** `static/images/galleries/2020/display/IMG_0998.HEIC.webp`

2020: ROB looked like one character, but inside he was already a team of
systems.

Structure carried the loads. Motion turned electrical energy into movement.
Sensors translated the world into numbers. Control software turned human intent
into bounded commands. Power connected all of it—and made safe design essential.

That is still one of our favorite ways to teach robotics: do not ask only,
"What does this part do?" Ask, "What other parts must cooperate before it can do
anything useful?"

Systems challenge: in this photo, can you point to one possible input and one
possible output?

## 4. 2021 — open the body and learn

**Suggested date:** September 11

**Suggested media:** `static/images/galleries/2021/display/IMG_1424.JPG.webp`

2021: opening ROB's body changed the engineering conversation.

The wiring bay, shoulder gear, arm, and cable loops all wanted the same limited
space. Suddenly, "make it fit" was not enough. A cable needed room to bend. A
moving joint needed clearance. A connector needed to be reachable again after
the panel went back on.

Serviceability is part of the design. If a system cannot be inspected, labeled,
or repaired, the next version becomes harder to understand.

If you opened this panel at the workbench, what would you label first?

## 5. 2022 — every new part creates six new questions

**Suggested date:** September 12

**Suggested media:** `static/images/galleries/2022/display/IMG_0171.HEIC.webp`

2022 was an integration year for ROB: computers, cameras, brackets, motor
hardware, wiring, and the tracked chassis all had to coexist.

Adding a component is never just "bolt it on." Where does its power come from?
How does data reach it? What carries the load? How is heat removed? Can it be
serviced? What makes it stop safely when communication disappears?

That is the real lesson behind an evolving robot. Progress is not the number of
parts installed. Progress is how well the parts cooperate—and how clearly the
remaining unknowns are documented.

## 7. 2024 — back at Mare Island

**Suggested date:** September 14

**Suggested media:** `assets/images/pages/Makerfaire_2024.webp`

2024 brought ROB to Historic Mare Island in Vallejo.

The best moments were not about claiming a robot could do everything. They were
the moments when someone leaned closer, noticed a gear or camera, and asked how
one system talked to another. A public demonstration turns years of workshop
decisions into a conversation.

Behind that conversation was another year of rebuilding: measuring the base,
opening the drivetrain, checking chain paths, packaging batteries and wiring,
and making the next revision easier to inspect.

We are aiming for Mare Island again on September 25–27, 2026. Who remembers ROB
from 2024?

## 8. 2025 — a chessboard is an engineering grid

**Suggested date:** September 17

**Suggested media:** `static/images/galleries/2025/display/IMG_6098.HEIC.webp`

2025: ROB faced a chessboard—but the real experiment was geometry.

A board gives the workshop a visible coordinate system. Cameras must agree on
where a square is. An arm must reach without colliding with the table, robot, or
people nearby. Software must distinguish a requested target from a measured
pose. And every movement still needs limits, supervision, and a reliable stop.

This photo records a vision-and-reach experiment, not a claim that ROB can play
autonomous chess. Honest boundaries make the project more useful because they
show exactly what must be tested next.

What safe tabletop task would you teach ROB first?
